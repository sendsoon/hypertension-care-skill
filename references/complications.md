# Complications and coexisting illness

Read this when hypertension sits beside another disease. The aim is to show how the illnesses and the medicines pull on each other, and what the person can watch at home. It is not a combined prescription.

Sources for blood-pressure targets remain [hypertension-guidelines.md](hypertension-guidelines.md). Disease-specific documents below were not re-quoted line by line on 2026-11-23. Use them as the place a clinician looks, and do not invent a recommendation class.

| Topic | Document to name, without pretending this file is that document |
|---|---|
| Heart failure | 2021 ESC heart-failure guidelines and the 2023 focused update; 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA heart-failure guideline |
| Kidney disease | KDIGO 2021 blood pressure in CKD; the current KDIGO CKD evaluation guideline |
| Diabetes | Current American Diabetes Association Standards of Care, which are revised yearly (https://diabetesjournals.org/care); do not freeze an old year |
| Stroke | Current AHA/ASA and ESO acute-stroke guidance. Acute blood-pressure numbers in stroke are emergency care, not home advice |
| Lipids | Current ACC/AHA or ESC/EAS lipid guidelines, and the Chinese lipid guideline the person's clinician uses |
| Gout | Current American College of Rheumatology gout guideline |

If a detail is not in the person's record and not in those documents as checked here, label it unverified.

## How to talk about two diseases at once

1. List what is clinician-confirmed, what is only patient-reported, and what is unknown.
2. Say which disease changes fluid, potassium, sodium, pulse, or fall risk.
3. Give a home step that does not alter prescriptions.
4. Name the one clinician question that would change the plan.
5. Do not rank the diseases into a treatment sequence.

## Coronary disease

**Clinical knowledge.** High blood pressure speeds atherosclerosis. Chest pain, chest pressure, sudden breathlessness, cold sweat, or pain into the arm, neck, or jaw can be an acute coronary syndrome even if blood pressure is "usual." That is emergency care.

Beta blockers, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, statins, and antiplatelet drugs may already be prescribed for the heart. Do not stop a beta blocker because the home reading looks fine. Nitrate dizziness plus a blood-pressure pill can drop standing pressure. Record both. Activity advice after a recent heart attack or stent belongs to cardiac rehabilitation, not to a generic walking plan. See [lifestyle-management.md](lifestyle-management.md).

## Heart failure

**Clinical knowledge.** Fluid advice that is right for an otherwise well person can be wrong here. Extra water, a high-salt restaurant meal, or an NSAID can cause congestion. Some people are on a fluid limit. Do not give a daily liter target unless the clinician wrote one.

Daily morning weight is a practical check when the clinician has asked for it. A rise of about 2 kg in three days, with more swelling or breathlessness, is a same-day clinician call in many heart-failure plans. Use the person's own written plan if they have one. Sudden severe breathlessness at rest is emergency care.

ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, evidence-based beta blockers, mineralocorticoid antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, and loop diuretics are clinician-directed. Non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers are generally avoided when ejection fraction is reduced. Do not add ibuprofen or a potassium salt substitute.

## Arrhythmia

**Clinical knowledge.** A new irregular, very fast, or very slow pulse, with dizziness, chest pain, or fainting, is urgent. A home blood-pressure monitor's pulse number is a clue, not an ECG diagnosis.

Beta blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, and some blood-pressure pills change rate and conduction. "My pulse is 50" needs the medicine list and symptoms before any comment. Do not tell the person to skip a beta blocker to raise the pulse.

Anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation is a bleeding-risk decision. Black stool, vomiting blood, or a serious fall with a head injury while on an anticoagulant is urgent care. Do not adjust warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant.

## Stroke and transient neurological symptoms

**Clinical knowledge.** Sudden face droop, arm or leg weakness, speech trouble, vision loss, severe unusual headache, or sudden loss of balance is a stroke until proven otherwise. Emergency services now. Do not give aspirin at home as a new idea, and do not try to lower blood pressure with an extra dose. Acute stroke targets are set in hospital and differ for thrombolysis, thrombectomy, and bleeding.

After a stroke, the blood-pressure target and the activity limit are the stroke team's. Home support is measurement, adherence, salt, and not missing rehabilitation. A new deficit is a new emergency, not "the old stroke."

## Chronic kidney disease

**Guideline recommendation.** KDIGO 2021 discusses a standardized office systolic target under 120 for many adults with CKD who are not on dialysis, and a different target after transplant. Chinese, ESC, and AHA/ACC targets are not the same number. See the guidelines file. Do not apply the KDIGO number to someone on dialysis.

**Clinical knowledge.** ACE inhibitors and ARBs are often continued for albuminuria unless potassium or creatinine becomes unsafe. That judgment needs labs. Vomiting, diarrhea, a fever, or a new NSAID is a danger period for the kidneys. A written sick-day plan from the clinician may pause certain drugs. If no written plan exists, call the clinician. Do not invent the pause.

Swelling, less urine, metallic taste, or confusion can be worsening kidney function. High-potassium foods and potassium salt substitutes are not default advice. Protein and fluid targets are individual.

## Diabetes

**Guideline recommendation.** Diabetes is a reason WHO, the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, and the Chinese guideline treat blood pressure more seriously than in a low-risk adult. The exact start threshold still differs by document. Use the clinician's target.

**Clinical knowledge.** ACE inhibitors or ARBs are often chosen when albumin is in the urine. They are not started in this chat. Beta blockers can blunt the warning of a low glucose. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists can lower pressure a little and can dehydrate a person who is also on a diuretic. Foot wounds, vision change, and chest pain are not lifestyle topics. Send them to care.

Do not set a carbohydrate gram target. Ask what the diabetes clinician already taught.

## Lipids and atherosclerosis

**Clinical knowledge.** Blood pressure and LDL cholesterol add risk. A statin already prescribed should not be stopped because of a muscle story without the prescriber, unless there is dark urine, severe weakness, or another emergency feature, in which case seek care and do not take the next dose until assessed. Grapefruit and some calcium-channel blockers interact with certain statins. The pharmacist can check the pair.

Leg pain on walking that stops with rest can be peripheral artery disease. An ankle-brachial index is a clinician's test. The 2024 Chinese guideline discusses limb blood-pressure differences as a clue. Do not diagnose a blocked artery from one cold foot.

## High uric acid and gout

**Clinical knowledge.** Thiazide diuretics can raise uric acid. A hot, swollen joint needs a clinician, because infection looks similar and is dangerous. Do not start colchicine, a uric-acid drug, or an NSAID as a blood-pressure workaround. Losartan's mild uric-acid effect is not a self-directed switch. Alcohol and sugary drinks are reasonable to cut during a flare. That does not treat the flare.

## Obesity and metabolic syndrome

**Clinical knowledge.** Weight loss can lower blood pressure, and the 2025 AHA/ACC lifestyle section treats it as a core measure. The Chinese guideline discusses BMI and waist targets for Chinese adults. Use those only as background. A safe pace for an older or frail person is slower, and it is not a crash diet. Sleep apnea, diabetes, and fatty-liver risk travel with central weight. Activity and plate changes are in [lifestyle-management.md](lifestyle-management.md). Do not prescribe weight-loss injections.

## Sleep apnea

**Guideline recommendation.** The 2025 AHA/ACC secondary-hypertension discussion lists obstructive sleep apnea as a common associated condition, especially when blood pressure is resistant. Snoring, witnessed pauses, morning headache, and daytime sleepiness are reasons to ask for a sleep assessment. They are not a home diagnosis.

**Individual suggestion.** Sleep on a schedule, avoid alcohol near bedtime, and do not add a sedative. Side sleeping is sometimes more comfortable. CPAP or a mouth device is prescribed after a study. Do not buy a "sleep supplement" for blood pressure.

## Osteoporosis and falls

**Clinical knowledge.** A fall can injure more than a high reading does, especially on an anticoagulant or in frail adults. Thiazides are associated in studies with slightly higher bone density. Loop diuretics are associated with more falls and, in some studies, fractures. Those associations are not a reason to swap diuretics here. Ask about vitamin D or bone drugs already prescribed. Do not start calcium mega-doses. Standing blood pressure plus strength and balance work, if the clinician allows activity, is the practical link. See [elderly-care.md](elderly-care.md).

## Frailty and cognition

**Clinical knowledge.** Intensive targets have evidence in selected older adults who could join trials. The Chinese 2024 text says that evidence is thin for dependent and institutionalized people. ESC 2024 loosens the target for moderate-to-severe frailty, age 85 and older, and symptomatic orthostatic hypotension.

New confusion, a fall, or a reading much lower than usual can be illness, dehydration, or too much combined drug effect. Seek care. Do not attribute a memory change to "just age" and do not attribute it to a blood-pressure pill without an assessment. Midlife hypertension is a long-term risk factor for later cognitive decline in observational work and in guideline discussions. That sentence must not become a threat, and it must not become a promise that a lower home reading will restore memory.

## What to record

For each coexisting illness: who confirmed it, the year if known, the current clinician, medicines used for it, and any written limit on fluid, potassium, activity, or blood-pressure target. If the person says "I have a weak kidney" without a number or a diagnosis, store that sentence as patient-reported and the diagnosis as not recorded.
