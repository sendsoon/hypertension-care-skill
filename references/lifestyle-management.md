# Lifestyle management

Read this for food, drink, movement, sleep, work, travel, and social meals. Advice must fit the record. A step that names a gram, a minute, or a plate is better than "eat well and keep happy."

If heart failure, reduced kidney function, edema, or an abnormal sodium or potassium is present or unknown, do not give a standard water volume or a potassium push. Read [complications.md](complications.md) first.

## Food

### Salt

**Guideline recommendation.** Aim toward under 5 g of salt a day (under 2 g of sodium) unless the clinician set a different limit.

**Individual suggestion.**

- Put the salt spoon down. A level teaspoon of table salt is roughly 5–6 g, about a whole day's limit. Cook the household pot without added salt, and let others add it at the table if they must.
- Soy sauce, oyster sauce, pickles, instant noodles, luncheon meat, and restaurant soup are salt, not "just flavor." On a restaurant day, skip the soup and the sauce dish, and do not add table salt.
- At a banquet, fill half the bowl with vegetables first, take one salty main dish, and leave the second serving.
- Taste changes over two to four weeks. Do not call a one-week slip a failure. Record it and return to the spoon at the next meal.
- A potassium-enriched salt substitute is a guideline option only when kidney function is known to be adequate and the person is not on an ACE inhibitor, ARB, ARNI, potassium-sparing diuretic, or a potassium pill, unless the clinician has approved it and is checking labs. If kidney status is unknown, do not recommend the substitute.

### DASH-style plate

**Guideline recommendation.** DASH emphasizes vegetables, fruit, low-fat dairy when tolerated, pulses, nuts, whole grains, and less saturated fat and added sugar. The 2025 AHA/ACC text also discusses dietary potassium of about 3500–5000 mg from food for people in whom it is safe. That food target is not a supplement order.

**Individual suggestion for a person without a fluid, potassium, or kidney restriction.**

- Vegetables at two meals, a closed fist or more each time.
- Fruit once or twice, whole fruit rather than juice.
- Protein from fish, skinless poultry, tofu, egg, or pulses more often than fatty red meat. Keep processed meat for rare days.
- Main grain in a bowl that is not heaped; prefer rice or noodles that are not fried, and add vegetables so the grain portion shrinks without an empty plate.
- Oil for the household pan, measured, rather than free pouring. The Chinese public slogan "less salt, less oil, less sugar" matches this. Do not invent a milliliter limit the person was not given.

**Do not use this plate** for a person told to limit potassium, fruit, fluid, or protein. Ask for that instruction first. One question: "Has a clinician told you to limit water, fruit, or salt substitute?"

### Water and drinks

- There is no universal daily water volume in this skill.
- Thirst, a dry mouth, and dark urine are cues to drink if the person is not on a fluid limit. Heart failure and some kidney disease need the clinician's cup limit.
- Coffee and tea are acceptable for many adults in moderate amounts. If a cup clearly raises pulse and the home readings that morning, shift caffeine earlier and tell the clinician. Do not ban tea for every older adult.
- Sugary drinks and alcohol-containing tonics are not blood-pressure treatment.
- Alcohol: prefer none, which matches both the Chinese guideline's preference and the AHA/ACC optimal goal. If the person drinks, name the Chinese ceiling (25 g alcohol for men, 15 g for women) as that guideline's upper bound, and say the AHA/ACC table reviewed here uses no more than 2 US drinks for men and 1 for women, with abstinence as the better goal. One Chinese white-spirit cup can exceed the day. Do not help the person "calculate a safe binge."

### Eating out and holidays

- Choose steamed, stewed, or cold dishes with a known sauce on the side. Ask for less salt when ordering. That request is the step.
- Share one salty dish among several people.
- On a festival day, decide the one meal that will be richer, eat smaller at the other meals, and measure blood pressure the next morning. Do not skip medicines to "make room" for the meal.
- A person who eats at canteens should look at the soup and the pickled side first. Skipping those two items cuts more sodium than swapping the grain.

### Older adults and appetite

A low-salt plan that leaves the person eating only plain rice is a harm. Keep protein foods they will actually eat, soften meat if chewing is hard, and use acid, ginger, garlic, and pepper for flavor when potassium salt is not allowed. Unplanned weight loss is a clinician question, not a success.

## Movement

**Guideline recommendation.** Build toward the WHO adult amounts if the clinician has not restricted activity. The 2025 AHA/ACC text says structured aerobic, dynamic resistance, and isometric work can lower blood pressure, and that lower-intensity walking is also useful. It describes structured exercise as generally safe, including for older adults with hypertension, inside the evidence it reviewed. That sentence does not clear a person with a recent heart attack, uncontrolled symptoms, or severe frailty.

**Operational rule.** Do not encourage high-intensity work while the person has chest pain, unexplained breathlessness, a resting reading in the severe range, a new neurological symptom, or a clinician's order to rest. No one should "sweat out" a reading at or above 180 systolic or 120 diastolic.

### A usable start

For a person who is currently inactive and has no symptom that forbids walking:

1. Warm up for 5 minutes by walking slowly indoors.
2. Walk at a pace where they can talk in sentences but not sing, for 10 minutes.
3. Slow down for 5 minutes. Sit before standing into a hot shower.
4. Repeat on 5 days. Add 5 minutes to the middle block when that week felt safe, up to 30 minutes.
5. Strength: sit-to-stand from a chair, 1 set of 5 to 8, two days a week, holding a table. Stop if dizzy.
6. Balance: stand at the kitchen counter and hold for 20 seconds, three days, only if they are steady enough not to fall. A person who already falls needs a clinician or physiotherapist before balance drills.
7. Flexibility: slow calf and shoulder stretches after the walk, no bouncing.

Tai chi, cycling on flat ground, and swimming are acceptable moderate options when the person already knows the skill and the clinician has not forbidden them. Keep the talk test. Do not upgrade a beginner to a fast road ride or to winter open-water swimming.

### Stop and seek care

Stop the session and seek urgent or emergency care, matching [emergency-warning.md](emergency-warning.md), for chest pain, breathlessness that does not settle with rest, fainting, new one-sided weakness, or a severe headache. Ordinary muscle tiredness can end the session without an emergency call. Tell the clinician at the next visit if it keeps happening.

## Sleep and mood

**Guideline recommendation.** The 2024 Chinese guideline suggests about 7–9 hours for adults and names sleep assessment and cognitive-behavioral treatment, with medicine only when needed and prescribed.

**Individual suggestion.**

- Fix the wake time first. Get daylight in the first hour. Keep caffeine to the morning if sleep is poor.
- The bed is for sleep. If awake for a long stretch, sit in dim light and return when sleepy. Do not use alcohol as a sleep aid.
- Snoring with pauses, morning headache, and daytime sleepiness: ask for a sleep evaluation. See [complications.md](complications.md).
- Worry about readings is common. A useful rule is to measure at the planned time, write the number, and not to repeat ten times unless the first pair differed by 10 mmHg or more or a symptom appeared. Extra anxious repeats are not more accurate.
- Low mood, loss of interest, or waking early with dread for more than two weeks is a reason to tell a clinician. This skill does not diagnose depression and does not start a psychiatric drug.
- Name one person the patient can call. A shared walk or a shared unsalted meal is a practical family step. Do not ask the family to police every bite.

## Work, travel, and social life

- **Desk work.** Stand and walk for 3 minutes each hour. Do the planned walk after work, not "when the quarter is over."
- **Physical labor.** Split loads, breathe while lifting, and do not lift to the point of breath-holding if blood pressure is uncontrolled. A hot worksite needs water and shade unless on a fluid limit, plus a midday reading if they feel light-headed.
- **Night shifts.** Measure before the main sleep and after waking, and keep medicine times anchored to the clinician's schedule rather than to the sun. Say in the record that times are shift-relative. Do not move a dose across a time zone or a shift change without the prescriber if the drug is taken more than once a day.
- **Long travel and flights.** Carry medicines in the cabin, in the original pack, with a spare day in the bag that stays with them. Sit only as the trip requires; walk the aisle or pump the ankles hourly. Do not take an extra dose because the clock changed. For a trip across several hours, ask the prescriber before departure how to place the next dose. Recent chest pain, severe heart failure, or a reading in the severe range is a reason to postpone non-essential travel.
- **Heat.** Vessels widen and diuretics dehydrate. Dizziness in a heat wave is a reason to sit, cool down, and call the clinician rather than to stop the pill. Never leave the monitor or the pills in a hot car.
- **Cold.** Blood pressure often runs higher. Measure indoors after warming up. Do not add a dose because the porch reading was high. Shoveling snow is a strain. People with coronary disease should not start it as exercise.
- **Meals with friends and retirement routines.** Keep one repeating weekly slot for the walk and one for the morning measurement. Retirement removes the commute but not the need for a clock. A regular market walk can be the activity plan if the route is safe and they can rest.

Write the chosen step in the log as the advice given, and write whether a fluid or potassium restriction made the standard plate unsafe.
