# Hypertension guidelines

Read this before interpreting a blood-pressure number, a category name, or a treatment target.

Checked against public guideline pages and texts on 2026-11-23. This is not a copy of those documents. If a newer edition exists, the newer edition wins. This skill does not set the person's target.

## How to label a statement

- **Guideline recommendation.** A recommendation in a named document, for a stated population.
- **Clinical knowledge.** A definition or mechanism widely used in practice. It is not an order for this person.
- **Individual suggestion.** Something fitted to the record. It still is not a prescription.
- **Unverified.** Missing, conflicting, or not found in the sources below. Say so. Do not fill the gap.

## What the numbers mean

Blood pressure is written systolic/diastolic in mmHg. Systolic is the pressure when the heart squeezes. Diastolic is the pressure when the heart relaxes.

One home or office reading does not diagnose hypertension and does not prove control. Major guidelines ask for repeated, standardized measurement, and they use home or ambulatory readings to separate sustained hypertension from white-coat and masked patterns.

### Categories differ on purpose

These are office categories for untreated adults, shortened. When systolic and diastolic fall in different grades, the higher grade is used. A person already treated for hypertension is still treated as having hypertension even if today's reading is lower. That last point is explicit in the 2024 Chinese guideline.

**Guideline recommendation.** Do not tell a person in China that an AHA stage-1 number is a Chinese diagnosis, or the reverse. Say which document the word came from.

### Patterns, not one disease

**Clinical knowledge**, used across these guidelines:

- **Primary hypertension.** No single removable cause found. This is the common situation.
- **Secondary hypertension.** A specific cause contributes, such as kidney disease, renal-artery disease, primary aldosteronism, sleep apnea, or a drug. Clues include onset at a young age, sudden worsening, resistant hypertension, and organ damage out of proportion to the history. The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline recommends screening for primary aldosteronism in resistant hypertension whether or not potassium is low. Screening is a clinician's test, not a home conclusion.
- **Older-age hypertension and isolated systolic hypertension.** Systolic pressure at or above 140 with diastolic under 90. Common when large arteries are stiffer. Diastolic pressure can be low. A very low diastolic reading is something the clinician balances against stroke and heart perfusion. Do not tell the person to push systolic down without that plan.
- **Resistant hypertension.** Blood pressure still above the agreed target on several appropriately dosed medicines, usually including a diuretic, after checking adherence, white-coat effect, and interfering drugs. The exact count of medicines should be taken from the clinician's definition. Do not label the person resistant from one bad week.
- **Masked hypertension.** Office readings look acceptable, home or ambulatory readings are high. Office-only reassurance can miss it.
- **White-coat hypertension.** Office readings are high, home or ambulatory readings are not. The 2025 AHA/ACC text says cardiovascular risk is higher for sustained hypertension than for white-coat hypertension, and that white-coat effect should be looked for with out-of-office readings. It also says office readings at or above 160/100 should be treated promptly rather than waiting on a white-coat explanation. Risk is not zero, especially in older adults who already have high cardiovascular risk.
- **Orthostatic hypotension.** ESC 2024: a drop of at least 20 mmHg systolic and/or 10 mmHg diastolic at 1 and/or 3 minutes after standing, checked after lying or sitting for 5 minutes. Consider it at diagnosis and again if symptoms appear. Symptoms include dizziness, dim vision, or near-fainting on standing.
- **Abnormal day-night pattern.** Ambulatory monitoring can show a missing night-time fall, or a night rise. The Chinese guideline treats night readings as part of diagnosis and of resistant hypertension. A home monitor in the morning does not describe the night. Do not invent a night pattern.
- **Hypertensive emergency and severe hypertension.** See [emergency-warning.md](emergency-warning.md). The defining feature of an emergency is acute organ injury, not a number alone. The 2025 AHA/ACC text uses blood pressure above 180/120 mmHg without acute organ injury as severe hypertension, and it retired the older phrase "hypertensive urgency."

## Treatment targets are not interchangeable

Quote a target only with its source and its exceptions. Then say that this person's target is the one their clinician set. If the record has no clinician target, write "Not recorded" and do not invent one.

**WHO 2021, pharmacological guideline, non-pregnant adults.**

- Start medicine at a confirmed systolic pressure at or above 140 or diastolic at or above 90.
- Also start for known cardiovascular disease with systolic 130–139.
- Suggested, not a strong recommendation: start for high cardiovascular risk, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease with systolic 130–139 and no known cardiovascular disease.
- Target under 140/90 if there are no comorbidities.
- Target systolic under 130 in known cardiovascular disease.
- Suggested systolic target under 130 for high cardiovascular risk, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease.
- First-line classes: thiazide or thiazide-like diuretic, ACE inhibitor or ARB, and long-acting dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker.
- Combination, preferably a single pill, is suggested as initial treatment. The implementation remark says combination is especially useful when the starting pressure is at least 20/10 above the target.
- Beta blockers are not in that first-line list.
- Monthly follow-up is suggested after a start or a change until the target is reached, then every 3–6 months. Certainty for the follow-up interval is low.

**ISH 2020, office targets in the optimal-care track.**

- Reduce by at least 20/10, ideally to under 140/90, within about 3 months, as the essential track.
- Under 65: under 130/80 if tolerated, but not to or below 120/70 in the optimal-target wording.
- 65 and older: under 140/90 if tolerated, individualized for frailty, independence, and tolerability.

**ESC 2024.**

- For most treated adults who tolerate it, the systolic target is 120–129, confirmed with out-of-office readings.
- A more lenient target is appropriate with symptomatic orthostatic hypotension, age 85 or older, moderate-to-severe frailty, or limited life expectancy. The guideline then uses "as low as reasonably achievable."
- Frailty should be considered with a validated check and a shared decision. This skill does not score frailty as a diagnosis.

**2025 AHA/ACC.**

- Lifestyle measures are recommended broadly, including weight, a DASH-style pattern, less sodium, more dietary potassium when safe, activity, stress care, and less or no alcohol.
- Medicine is recommended at an average at or above 140/90, and for selected adults at or above 130/80 who have clinical cardiovascular disease, prior stroke, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or a 10-year PREVENT cardiovascular risk at or above 7.5 percent.
- Adults without those conditions and with PREVENT risk under 7.5 percent are recommended to start medicine if the average remains at or above 130/80 after 3 to 6 months of lifestyle treatment.
- The evidence discussion supports a systolic goal under 130 for many adults. A goal under 120 is discussed from a smaller set of trials and is not an order for every person.
- For older adults who are frail or have a limited life expectancy, the guideline tells the clinician and the patient to weigh benefit and harm. It does not tell this skill to pick the number.
- PREVENT is a US risk equation for adults 30–79 without prior cardiovascular disease. Do not present a PREVENT percent for a person whose inputs you do not have, and do not treat it as the Chinese risk grade.

**2024 Chinese guideline, office targets as summarized in the full text.**

- High or very high cardiovascular risk, or hypertension with comorbidities: under 130/80 if tolerated.
- Hypertension without comorbidities: under 140/90, and under 130/80 if tolerated.
- Ages 65–79: under 140/90, and under 130/80 if tolerated.
- Age 80 and older: under 150/90, and under 140/90 if tolerated.
- The text says age alone is not a reason to keep the target high, and that tolerability, comorbidities, and the ability to stay on treatment decide the intensity.
- It also says evidence is still limited for institutionalized, dependent, frail, and very old adults.
- Most people are brought to target over about 4 to 12 weeks. Faster is more reasonable in younger people with a short history. Slower is more reasonable in older people, a long history, comorbidities, and poor tolerance.
- The same guideline keeps grade-3 hypertension because it remains common in China. Do not drop that grade when speaking with a person whose care follows the Chinese guideline.

**KDIGO 2021, chronic kidney disease not treated with dialysis.**

- For many adults, standardized office systolic target under 120.
- Adult kidney-transplant recipients: systolic under 130 and diastolic under 80.
- The target is tied to standardized measurement, not to a rushed single clinic reading.
- Dialysis blood pressure is outside this recommendation. Do not copy the under-120 target onto a person on dialysis.

### Why the skill will not choose among these

A systolic target of under 120 (KDIGO, selected trials), 120–129 (ESC 2024), under 130 (WHO for some groups, AHA/ACC, Chinese high-risk if tolerated), and under 140 or under 150 (older or frailer groups in ISH and the Chinese guideline) can all be "correct" for different people. Name the conflict. Ask what the treating clinician said. Record that sentence as clinician-confirmed only if the person is reporting the clinician's words. Otherwise label the guideline list as an AI-generated assessment and the personal target as not recorded.

Trials that moved intensive targets, including SPRINT (Williamson and others' older-adult analyses and the main NEJM 2015 report, DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa1511939) and the STEP trial in older Chinese adults (NEJM, 2021), are evidence behind guidelines. They are not a home order to seek a trial target.

## Measurement the person can do

**Guideline recommendation, shared practical core.** Use an upper-arm device that has passed a validation protocol. The 2024 Chinese guideline recommends a validated upper-arm electronic monitor and advises against a mercury monitor.

**Individual suggestion, unless the clinician gave a different routine.**

1. Sit with the back supported, feet on the floor, legs uncrossed, and the cuff at heart height. Use the cuff size that fits the arm.
2. Do not exercise, smoke, or drink caffeine for 30 minutes before. Empty the bladder. Rest quietly for 5 minutes. Do not talk during the reading.
3. Take at least two readings. The 2024 Chinese guideline shortened the gap between repeated readings to 30–60 seconds, and uses a third reading when the first two differ by 10 mmHg or more. If the person's clinician taught a different gap, keep the clinician's method and say which one is in use.
4. Write systolic, diastolic, pulse, time, arm, position, and any symptom. Morning readings before medicine and evening readings are the usual pair. The Chinese diagnosis pathway asks for several consecutive days, not one day.
5. Measure standing blood pressure, after sitting or lying for 5 minutes, at 1 and 3 minutes, when the person is older, dizzy on standing, has diabetes, or has had a fall. Record both positions.
6. Wrist and finger devices are a last resort. If a clinician has already matched a wrist device to an upper-arm device, say that limit in the record. Do not treat an unvalidated watch as a diagnosis.

## What belongs to the clinician

Starting medicine, the drug class, a two-drug start, labs before or after a change, a search for a secondary cause, and any intensive target. WHO suggests labs and risk assessment in its 2021 guideline rather than delaying care forever for a perfect work-up. The exact panel is ordered by the clinician. Common panels across these guidelines include kidney function, electrolytes, glucose, lipids, urine, and an ECG. Record only tests the person or a report actually provides.

Children and pregnancy use different thresholds. Do not apply the adult tables. For pregnancy, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and ARNIs are avoided because of fetal harm. That is a reason to speak to the obstetric clinician, not a reason for this skill to choose another drug.
